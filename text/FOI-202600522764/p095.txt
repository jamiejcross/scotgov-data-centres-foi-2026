It’s excellent to see that such clear and thoughtful analysis is going into the impacts,
both positive and negative, of data centres both nationally and locally. Thank you for
sharing them.



Having looked at the documents, there are clearly signiﬁcant impacts and risks and,
considering our experience of just transition work over the last few years, I have various
concerns about the way forward.



The ﬁrst is the part that talks about the likelihood of rising energy bills for locals. There is
a clear just transition risk here, considering the prevalence of fuel poverty in the country
already- and I’m conscious that is about to get worse as energy bills rise in the wake of
the middle east conﬂict. An example that is often highlighted to us as an example of
unjust transition, is that of Shetland, where there is extensive wind development
o shore (which can disrupt other activities like ﬁshing) and yet locals have some of the
highest electricity bills and fuel poverty in the country. Depending on location, there is a
clear risk here that locals bear the brunt of increased electricity prices- or even a
shortage of energy, or unreliable power- while seeing little direct beneﬁt from ‘hosting’
the data centre. I would suggest that there needs to be a really clear system for
protecting locals from increased energy prices and power shortages, as part of a deal to
host the centre. That’s not just about grid upgrades, it’s about demand and supply.



The second is water. I appreciate the description below, where part of being a green
data centre is about e icient and minimal use of water- and that’s welcome. The hard
fact is that, even taking data centres completely out of the equation, Scotland is facing
water shortages in multiple and growing parts of the country, due to climate change.
Last year, water shortages in some parts of the country started in April- the earliest ever.
And some rivers are already under strain from multiple industries (renewable energy,
whisky, agriculture) so that ﬁsheries, biodiversity and the environment are destroyed
downstream. Those e ects are already set to become much worse. Add a water-hungry
data centre and, even if it is comparatively e icient, it could badly impact other water-
dependent industries and livelihoods. I note in the slides that in Australia, data centre
construction is dependent on guarantees of proportional work like reservoir investment
and other management. That seems like a really fruitful route to explore. I would also
suggest that planning and impacts assessments take account of the latest Met O ice
assessments of weather changes to come- the Met O ice provide predictions mapped
to a local level, up to at least 2050. When planning, we shouldn’t erroneously start from
a baseline of current water availability, but we should build in future increasing
shortages.
